## Pricing Models Explained

### Revenue Share

The vendor takes a percentage of your gross gaming revenue instead of charging upfront fees. Sounds operator-friendly because you only pay when players lose money. Reality is more complex.

**Pros:**
- Low barrier to entry - minimal upfront investment
- Vendor incentive alignment with operator growth
- Cash flow matches revenue generation
- Easier budget approval for startups

**Cons:**
- Becomes expensive at scale with no ceiling
- Harder to forecast costs as you grow
- Vendor has ongoing claim on your highest-margin revenue
- Less negotiating power once you're generating significant GGR

**Best for:** Startups testing market viability, operators entering new jurisdictions, brands without access to significant development capital.

**Worst for:** High-volume operators who understand their unit economics - once you're doing $1M+ monthly GGR, the math usually favors fixed licensing.

**Typical category range:** 8-20% of net gaming revenue, with most deals settling between 10-15%. The rate often tiers down as your volume increases, but the absolute amount you pay keeps growing.

### License Fee

You pay a fixed monthly or annual fee for platform access, regardless of your revenue performance. The vendor makes money on the license, not your success.

**Pros:**
- Predictable cost structure for financial planning
- Lower total cost at scale compared to revenue share
- No ongoing claim on your revenue growth
- Better margins as your operation grows

**Cons:**
- High upfront capital requirement
- Full cost burden during launch phase with no revenue
- Vendor has less incentive to support your growth
- Higher financial risk if market entry fails

**Best for:** Well-capitalized operators with clear volume projections, multi-market launches where revenue share would compound costs, operators prioritizing margin preservation at scale.

**Worst for:** Startups burning investor capital with unclear time-to-profitability, single-market tests where failure means total loss.

**Typical category range:** $15K-$100K+ monthly depending on included modules and market coverage. Enterprise deals with full regulatory coverage can run $200K+ monthly.

### Hybrid Models

Combines reduced revenue share with smaller upfront license fees. Attempts to balance cash flow management with cost predictability.

**Pros:**
- Lower upfront cost than pure licensing
- Reduced revenue share compared to pure rev-share deals
- Some vendor alignment with operator success
- More flexibility in deal structure

**Cons:**
- Complex cost forecasting with multiple variables
- Neither the cash flow benefits of rev-share nor cost predictability of licensing
- Harder to compare against pure-model competitors
- Often includes hidden triggers that change pricing

**Best for:** Mid-market operators with established revenue but growth capital constraints, multi-brand operators spreading platform costs across properties.

**Worst for:** Operators who prefer simple deal structures, startups that need pure cash-flow alignment, enterprises that can optimize with pure licensing.

**Typical category range:** 3-8% revenue share plus $10K-$50K monthly license, with the mix varying based on operator preferences and scale projections.

Pure revenue share favors operators who don't know if they'll succeed. Pure licensing favors operators who know they will. Hybrid models exist because most operators fall somewhere between those extremes, but they often deliver the worst of both approaches rather than the best.

## Budget Anatomy - Where the Money Actually Goes

The headline platform number covers maybe 40% of your real costs. Here's where the rest goes:

- **Platform license or rev share:** Core PAM, basic content aggregation, standard reporting, tier-1 support during business hours. What's not included: premium game content deals, payment gateway relationships, compliance automation, weekend support, custom development, data migration tooling.

- **Implementation and onboarding:** Vendor quotes assume standard deployment with their preferred integration partners and minimal customization. Reality: custom UI work adds 6-12 weeks, additional market certifications add cost and timeline, content migration from existing platforms requires vendor professional services, multi-jurisdiction launches multiply compliance work. "Turnkey in 4 weeks" typically means 12-16 weeks when you account for payment testing, game certification, and regulatory sign-offs.

- **Payment processing:** Gateway fees run 2-4% of deposits, FX markups add another 1-2% on international transactions, chargeback handling costs $15-50 per dispute, some payment providers demand their own revenue share on top of transaction fees. Alternative payment methods (crypto, e-wallets, bank transfers) each require separate integrations and fee structures.

- **Game content and provider fees:** Game studio revenue share stacks on top of platform revenue share - typically 15-25% to content providers. Premium content (live dealer, exclusive games, branded slots) carries higher rates. Some studios require minimum revenue guarantees. New game releases often have promotional fee structures that increase after launch periods.

- **Compliance and regulatory:** Per-jurisdiction costs that compound as you expand markets - responsible gaming tooling runs $5K-$20K monthly per market, audit support fees for license renewals, regulatory reporting automation, AML monitoring tools, player verification services, tax reporting systems. Brazil's new regulatory framework adds specific compliance costs for certified operators.

- **Infrastructure and hosting:** CDN costs for global content delivery, redundancy and disaster recovery systems, peak traffic handling during major sporting events, DDoS protection, SSL certificates, monitoring and alerting systems. Cloud hosting scales with user load but can spike unexpectedly.

- **Support tiers:** Standard support typically covers business hours in vendor timezone. Premium support (24/7/365, dedicated account management, priority issue escalation, weekend deployment support) runs $10K-$50K monthly depending on service level commitments.

- **Data migration and exit costs:** Often more expensive than onboarding - player data export, transaction history migration, compliance audit trails, payment method migration, bonus liability transfer. Some vendors charge extraction fees; others make the technical process difficult enough to discourage switching.

Operators consistently underestimate payment processing, game provider fees, and compliance automation in their launch budgets. The headline platform number gets you the technology foundation, but operational costs accumulate as you add markets, payment methods, and content variety.

By the time the absolute cost becomes painful, you're locked into contracts that run 2-3 years, and migration costs make switching prohibitive. Smart operators negotiate rev-share caps or conversion options into their initial contracts, before they have leverage.

## Hidden Costs Operators Underestimate

- **Payment provider revenue share stacking:** Some payment companies take their own percentage of GGR on top of transaction fees, separate from your platform provider's revenue share. You end up paying 15% to your platform plus 8% to your payment partner, and nobody mentioned the 8% during platform selection.

- **Game studio minimum guarantees:** Popular content providers require revenue guarantees regardless of player activity. You're committed to paying $10K monthly to NetEnt whether players touch their games or not. These commitments stack across multiple studios and aren't always disclosed in platform pricing discussions.

- **Compliance automation per jurisdiction:** Each new market requires separate compliance tooling, reporting systems, and audit capabilities. What looks like a $50K annual compliance budget for one market becomes $200K when you expand to four markets, with limited economies of scale.

- **Peak traffic infrastructure costs:** Your hosting costs are typically calculated on average usage, but major sporting events, jackpot wins, or marketing campaigns can drive 5-10x normal traffic. Peak handling infrastructure sits idle most of the time but you pay for it constantly.

- **Premium support billing creep:** Standard support works fine until you have an outage during Champions League final weekend. Premium support upgrades often get added mid-contract when operators realize 24-hour email response times don't work for live operations.

- **Data retention and storage costs:** Player transaction histories, game round data, audit trails, and compliance records accumulate continuously. Storage costs grow linearly with operational time, and some jurisdictions require 7+ years of data retention.

- **Exit clause implementation fees:** The contract says you can leave with 90 days notice, but data extraction, integration disconnection, and audit trail preservation require vendor professional services at $200-300 hourly rates. Six-month exit processes aren't uncommon.

Negotiate these categories INTO your contract before signing, not after - once the contract is signed, every addition becomes a billable change request with vendor-favorable terms.

## Negotiation Levers

- **Multi-year commitment with declining rates:** Three-year deals typically unlock 15-25% better pricing than annual contracts. Revenue share can tier down from 12% in Year 1 to 8% in Year 3, or license fees can include annual escalation caps below inflation rates.

- **Multi-jurisdiction expansion options:** Negotiate pricing for additional markets during initial contract discussions. "If we expand to three markets within 18 months, what does Market 2 and 3 pricing look like?" Lock in expansion rates before you have leverage, not after you're successful.

- **Volume threshold triggers:** Build revenue milestones that automatically improve your deal terms. "At $2M monthly GGR, revenue share drops to 8%" or "License fee caps at $75K monthly regardless of scale." These clauses protect you from your own success.

- **Technology refresh and migration credits:** Platform technology evolves fast. Negotiate credits for major platform upgrades, API modernization, or migration to new infrastructure. Include specific commitments around backward compatibility and transition support.

- **Co-marketing and case study value:** Vendors need reference clients for sales purposes. If you're launching in a new market, targeting an interesting demographic, or achieving notable growth metrics, that has value in their sales process. Trade visibility for better commercial terms.

- **Payment and compliance bundling:** Instead of negotiating platform pricing separately from payment processing and compliance tooling, bundle them into single deals with consolidated pricing. Vendors often have partnership arrangements that can deliver better total cost.

- **Professional services caps:** Implementation always takes longer than quoted. Negotiate fixed-price onboarding packages or cap professional services at hourly rates. "Custom development not to exceed $50K without written approval" prevents scope creep from destroying your budget.

Frame negotiations around risk sharing and partnership value, not just price optimization. Vendors prefer operators who understand the business model and can articulate mutual benefit rather than purely extracting concessions.

## Red Flags in Pricing Conversations

- **"Industry standard" pricing claims without specifics:** Any vendor who says their rates are "standard for the industry" without explaining their calculation or showing comparison points is probably overcharging. Standard according to whom? For what scale of operation? Including which services?

- **Bundled pricing that obscures per-component costs:** When vendors refuse to break down what you're paying for individual modules, payment processing, compliance tooling, and support services, they're usually subsidizing weak components with strong ones and preventing you from optimizing.

- **Custom pricing with no ballpark estimates:** Professional vendors can give rough ranges based on similar client profiles even when final pricing requires detailed scoping. "Every deal is different" without any guidance suggests poor sales process or intentional opacity.

- **Critical costs that only emerge after contract signing:** Payment processing fees, compliance tool requirements, hosting infrastructure, premium support needs, and integration complexity should be discussed during initial conversations, not discovered during implementation.

- **Revenue guarantees or minimum GGR commitments buried in contract terms:** Some deals include minimum revenue commitments that trigger penalties if you don't hit targets. These clauses need to be front-and-center during negotiations, not buried in contract appendices.

- **"All-inclusive" pricing that excludes major operational necessities:** Turnkey solutions that don't include payment processing, game content licensing, compliance tooling, or customer support aren't actually turnkey. Know what's included and what gets billed separately.

- **Vendor unwillingness to discuss contract exit procedures:** Professional vendors should explain data portability, integration disconnection, and transition support processes upfront. Reluctance to discuss exits suggests they're counting on lock-in effects rather than service quality for retention.

These red flags don't necessarily disqualify vendors, but they indicate you need deeper diligence on total cost structure and contract terms before making decisions.

## Common Questions

### Why won't platform vendors publish pricing openly?

Because pricing depends on variables that can't be standardized - your projected scale, market focus, technical requirements, timeline, risk profile, and negotiating position. A startup testing one market pays different rates than an enterprise expanding globally. Published prices would either be meaningless averages or maximum rates that scare away prospects.

More practically, custom pricing lets vendors optimize deals for different client types. They can offer better rates to strategic accounts, adjust terms based on competitive situations, and structure deals around cash flow preferences. Published pricing removes that flexibility and commoditizes their service.

### At what revenue threshold does revenue share become more expensive than licensing?

Typically between $1M-$3M monthly GGR, depending on the specific rates and what's included in each model. An operator doing $2M monthly at 12% revenue share pays $2.9M annually in platform fees. Most enterprise license deals for equivalent functionality run $1M-$2M annually.

The crossover point varies based on deal structure, included services, and total cost of ownership. Some license deals require separate payment processing, compliance tooling, and premium support that revenue share deals include. Run the full cost calculation, not just headline rates.

### What's the realistic Year 1 total cost versus the headline platform number?

Platform fees typically represent 30-50% of total technology costs in Year 1. A $100K annual platform license becomes $250K-$300K when you add payment processing, game content, compliance tooling, hosting, premium support, and implementation services.

Revenue share deals can look more affordable initially but include fewer services. A 12% revenue share might seem cheaper than $100K licensing until you add separate payment processing (3-4% of GGR), compliance automation ($2K-$5K monthly), hosting infrastructure, and professional services for custom integration work.

### How do I compare vendor proposals when pricing structures are completely different?

Build total cost models at specific revenue scenarios rather than comparing headline rates. Model Year 1, Year 2, and Year 3 costs at conservative, expected, and aggressive growth projections. Include platform fees, payment processing, game content, compliance tools, hosting, and support in each scenario.

Revenue share looks attractive at low volumes but becomes expensive at scale. License fees require upfront investment but offer better margins later. Hybrid models provide middle ground but add forecasting complexity. Choose the structure that aligns with your capital availability and growth confidence.

### What contract terms protect operators from unexpected cost escalations?

Annual price increase caps (typically 3-5% maximum regardless of inflation), revenue share tier-down structures that improve rates as you scale, professional services hourly rate caps with approval requirements for overages, and specific inclusion/exclusion lists for what triggers additional billing.

Most importantly, negotiate expansion pricing during initial contracts rather than waiting until you need additional markets, payment methods, or service levels. Your leverage is highest before you're operationally dependent on the platform.

### How do payment processing costs interact with platform pricing?

Payment processing typically runs 2-5% of gross gaming revenue as a separate cost layer from platform fees. Some vendors include payment orchestration in their platform pricing but charge separately for gateway relationships. Others require you to establish direct relationships with payment providers.

The complexity: some payment companies also take revenue share percentages on top of transaction fees, creating double revenue sharing that can push total platform costs above 20% of GGR. Understand who gets paid what percentage of your revenue before committing to any platform deal.
