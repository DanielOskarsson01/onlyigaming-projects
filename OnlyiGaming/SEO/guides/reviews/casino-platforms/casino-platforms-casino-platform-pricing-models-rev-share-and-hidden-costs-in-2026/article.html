Pricing Models Explained

The casino platform market runs on four main commercial models. Each one shifts risk and cost timing differently.

Revenue Share

Revenue share means the platform takes a percentage of your gross gaming revenue (GGR) monthly. Typical ranges are 10-25%, with most deals landing between 12-18%.

Pros:

  • Lower upfront capital requirement
  • Platform success aligned with operator success
  • Easier to budget as a percentage of revenue
  • Less financial risk during launch phase

Cons:

  • Becomes expensive at scale (millions in annual fees for successful brands)
  • Platform controls the billing metric (GGR calculation)
  • Harder to predict long-term unit economics
  • Creates incentive misalignment around operator margins

Best for: New operators, crypto brands, or anyone launching in unproven markets where upfront capital preservation matters more than long-term cost optimization.

Fixed License Fee

Monthly or annual licensing fees regardless of revenue performance. Enterprise deals often combine multiple modules into bundled pricing.

Pros:

  • Predictable cost structure for financial planning
  • No revenue sharing means better unit economics at scale
  • More negotiation leverage on renewal terms
  • Cleaner margin calculations for investors

Cons:

  • High upfront and ongoing costs regardless of performance
  • Platform has less incentive to support your growth
  • Cash flow pressure during low-revenue periods
  • Typically requires minimum commitment terms

Best for: Established operators with predictable revenue streams, multi-brand portfolios, or businesses targeting high-volume markets where revenue share costs become prohibitive.

Hybrid Models

Combination of lower revenue share (5-10%) plus monthly platform fees. Some deals include performance tiers that shift the ratio as you scale.

Pros:

  • Balances upfront cost management with long-term economics
  • Platform maintains growth incentive without maximum cost exposure
  • Can include volume discounts or performance bonuses
  • Flexibility to renegotiate terms based on actual performance

Cons:

  • More complex contract terms and billing
  • Requires accurate revenue forecasting for budget planning
  • Platform may push you toward the higher-margin pricing tier
  • Exit costs can be higher due to multiple fee components

Best for: Mid-market operators with established revenue but growth plans, or businesses entering multiple markets with different performance expectations.

Transaction-Based Pricing

Payment per game round, bet, or player action. Less common for full platforms but used for specific modules like payment orchestration or fraud prevention.

Pros:

  • Direct cost correlation with platform usage
  • Easy to calculate ROI per transaction
  • No revenue sharing or minimum commitments
  • Scales perfectly with business volume

Cons:

  • Can become expensive for high-frequency games
  • Billing complexity for multiple transaction types
  • Less predictable monthly costs
  • May incentivize platform to increase transaction volume regardless of profitability

Best for: Operators with specific module needs rather than full platform requirements, or businesses with highly variable transaction patterns.

Red Flags in Pricing Conversations

Watch for these warning signs during vendor pricing discussions:

  • "Custom pricing only" with no ballpark range: Legitimate vendors can give you rough ranges based on your described requirements. Refusing to provide any guidance usually means they're planning to price based on your perceived budget rather than actual cost.

  • Setup fees that exceed 50% of annual licensing cost: High setup fees often hide poor implementation processes or understaffed onboarding teams. If it costs $200K to set up a $300K annual license, question whether their platform is actually implementation-ready.

  • Revenue share calculations that exclude certain game types: Some deals exclude live dealer, progressive jackpots, or specific providers from revenue share calculations, then charge separate fees for those games. Read the fine print on what counts toward your revenue share base.

  • Bundled pricing that obscures per-module costs: All-in-one pricing makes it impossible to evaluate cost per capability or negotiate specific component changes. This pricing strategy often hides weak modules behind strong ones.

  • Minimum GGR commitments buried in contract terms: Some deals require minimum monthly revenue commitments regardless of actual performance. Miss the minimum, and you pay the difference as a penalty fee.

  • Payment processing markups disguised as "platform fees": Platform providers sometimes add 1-2% markup on payment processing costs without clearly disclosing this as a profit center separate from the core platform license.

The best pricing conversations include detailed cost breakdowns, clear examples of additional fees, and specific timelines with milestone-based payment schedules.
